# The Bookkeeper

@year 1839 | Richford, New York
John Davison Rockefeller was born in 1839, in a small town in upstate New York.

@duo His father;A travelling salesman;of doubtful medicines | His mother;Strict, religious,;careful with every cent
His father, William, was a travelling salesman who sold doubtful medicines, and called himself a doctor.
He was charming, he was unreliable, and he was often gone for months at a time.
His mother, Eliza, was strict, religious, and careful with every cent.
The boy took after his mother.

@words He cheated his own sons, | to make them *sharp.*
He liked to boast that he cheated his own sons in small deals, whenever he could. He said it would make them sharp.

@words His father's *second life* | A different name. | A second wife.
Years later, it emerged that his father had been living a second life, under a different name, with a second wife.
It was a secret the family kept for decades.

@art coins | A boy who studied money
Even as a child, John studied money the way other boys studied games.
He raised turkeys and sold them. He bought candy by the pound, and sold it to his brothers and sisters by the piece.

@stat $ | 50 | | lent to a farmer, at interest
When he had saved $50, he lent it to a local farmer, at interest.
A year later the money came back, with more attached, and he had done nothing for it.
He later said this was the moment he decided to let money work for him, instead of working for money.

@year 1853 | The family moves to Ohio
In 1853 the family moved west, to Ohio. John finished school in Cleveland, and then took a three month course in bookkeeping.

@words Six days a week. | Every firm in the city. | For *weeks.*
In the summer of 1855, sixteen years old, he went looking for work.
He walked the streets of Cleveland six days a week, in a dark suit, calling on every firm that might need a clerk.
When he reached the end of his list, he started again at the top.

@art ledger | Hewitt and Tuttle · 26 September 1855
On the twenty-sixth of September, a small trading firm called Hewitt and Tuttle gave him a job as an assistant bookkeeper.
He loved it. He loved the columns of figures, and the way a ledger showed exactly where every dollar went.

@words *26 September* | His personal holiday, | every year, for life.
For the rest of his life he celebrated that date as a personal holiday. He called it Job Day.

@stat $ | 50 | | his pay for the first three months
Nobody told him what he would be paid. After three months, he was handed $50.

@words He checked *every bill.* | And caught the errors | nobody else looked for.
At Hewitt and Tuttle, he checked every bill that crossed his desk, line by line. He caught overcharges that older men had waved through.
Within a few years, he was doing the work of a senior man. He asked for a salary of $800 a year. The firm offered $700.
So he left, and started his own.

@words Every cent *earned.* | Every cent *spent.* | Every cent *given.*
He kept a ledger of his own. Every cent earned, every cent spent, every cent given away.
Even on that small wage, he gave a regular share of it to his church.
It was a habit he never dropped.

@art church | The Erie Street Baptist Church
His church mattered to him as much as his work. When it was about to lose its building over a debt of $2,000, he stood at the door after the service, and asked every member, one by one, for a pledge.
He wrote each promise in a notebook. Within months, the debt was paid. He was barely twenty years old.

@year 1859 | Clark and Rockefeller
In 1859, at nineteen, he went into business for himself, with a partner named Maurice Clark.
They traded grain, hay and meat. To raise his share, he borrowed $1,000 from his father, who charged him ten percent interest.

@art barrel | The war years
When the Civil War came, the firm supplied food to the Union army, and the profits climbed.
Rockefeller did not fight. Like many men of means, he paid for substitutes to serve in his place.

@duo First ambition;To make;$100,000 | Second ambition;To live;to 100
He is said to have had two ambitions in life. To make $100,000. And to live to be a hundred.
He would miss one of them. By three years.

@year 1859 | About a hundred miles away
And in that same year, 1859, something happened about a hundred miles away that would change his life.

# Black Gold

@art candle | Before oil
In the 1850s, when the sun went down, most of the world went dark.
The best light came from whale oil, and it was expensive. Everyone else made do with candles, or with cheap fuels that had a habit of exploding.

@words A black, sticky oil | that seeped out of the ground | in *Pennsylvania.*
In western Pennsylvania, a black, sticky oil seeped out of the ground and floated on the creeks.
A few investors believed it could be refined into a cheap, bright lamp fuel. The problem was getting enough of it.

@art derrick | Edwin Drake · Titusville, Pennsylvania
They hired a former railroad conductor named Edwin Drake, and sent him to the village of Titusville.
To impress the locals, they addressed his mail to Colonel Drake. He had never been a colonel.

@words They called it | *Drake's Folly.*
Drake decided to drill for oil, the way men drilled for salt. The locals thought he was mad. They called the project Drake's Folly.

@year 1859 | 27 August · oil at 69 feet
He hired a blacksmith to do the drilling. Progress was slow, and the money ran out.
Then, on the twenty-seventh of August, 1859, at a depth of about sixty-nine feet, the drill slipped into a crevice.
The next day, there was oil floating at the top of the pipe.

@art pipeline | Drake's one lasting idea
Drake left one lasting idea behind. When water kept collapsing the hole, he drove an iron pipe down into the ground, and drilled inside it.
Drillers still use the same principle today. He never patented it.

@words The first *oil rush* | in America.
It was the start of the first oil rush in America.

@art lamp | Oil meant light
At the time, oil was not about cars. There were no cars. Oil meant light.
Refined into kerosene, it burned brighter and cheaper than anything before it, and it let ordinary families light their homes after dark.

@art derrick | The Oil Region
Thousands of people poured into the valley. Farmers became rich overnight. Derricks went up so close together that they nearly touched.
There were not enough barrels to hold the oil, so men used whiskey barrels. That is why oil is still measured in barrels today.

@stat | 19 | | people killed in a single well fire, 1861
The work was dangerous. In 1861, a gushing well caught fire, and killed nineteen people.

@words Even the man who shot | *Abraham Lincoln* | had invested in oil.
The fever touched everyone. Even the actor John Wilkes Booth put money into an oil company, a year before he shot Abraham Lincoln.

@bars $# a barrel | Start of 1861:10 | End of 1861:0.10
But nobody controlled the supply. When a new well came in, the price collapsed.
In a single year, a barrel of oil is said to have fallen from around $10 to as little as ten cents.

@words Pithole, Pennsylvania | *15,000 people* in months. | Almost empty in years.
One town, called Pithole, grew from a farm to a city of about 15,000 people in a matter of months.
A few years later, the wells ran dry, and it was almost empty.

@art pipeline | The first pipeline, 1865
Getting the oil out was its own battle. Barrels were hauled over mud roads by wagon drivers called teamsters, who charged whatever they liked.
In 1865, a man named Samuel Van Syckel built a pipeline, about five miles long, from the wells to the railroad. The teamsters tried to tear it up. It had to be protected by armed guards.
But it worked. And it was a sign of things to come. Whoever controlled the movement of oil would control the business.

@words Fortunes made *in a week.* | Lost *in a day.*
Fortunes were made in a week, and lost in a day.
Even Edwin Drake, the man who started it all, ended up poor, living on a small pension from the state.

@art refinery | How refining worked
Refining was simple in principle. Crude oil was heated in a large still, and its different parts boiled off at different temperatures.
The lightest part, gasoline, was too explosive for lamps. The middle part was kerosene. What was left was heavy tar.
A small refinery could be built for a few thousand dollars. Which is why so many people tried.

@duo Drilling;A gamble | Refining;A business
Rockefeller visited the oil fields, and looked at the chaos with a bookkeeper's eye.
He saw that drilling was a gamble. But whoever turned crude oil into kerosene was running a business.

@art train | Cleveland: the lake and the railroads
And Cleveland was a good place to do it. It sat on a great lake, and it was served by several competing railroads.
A refiner in Cleveland could play one carrier against another.

@stat | 30 | | refineries in Cleveland by 1865
By the middle of the 1860s, there were about thirty refineries in Cleveland alone.

@year 1863 | His first refinery, Cleveland
In 1863, he and his partners invested in a refinery, with an English chemist named Samuel Andrews, who knew how to run one.
Within two years, the refinery was earning more than the trading business.

@duo Rockefeller;Borrow, build,;expand | The Clarks;Slow down,;stay safe
But the partners quarrelled. Rockefeller wanted to borrow heavily and expand. The Clark brothers thought he was reckless.
In February 1865, they agreed to settle it with an auction, among themselves. The highest bidder would keep the business.

@stat $ | 72500 | | to buy out his partners, at auction, in 1865
The bidding started at $500. It climbed, and kept climbing.
When the Clarks reached $72,000, Rockefeller, without hesitating, bid $72,500.
The Clarks gave up.

@words Twenty-five years old. | His own *master.*
He was twenty-five years old, and he now controlled one of the largest refineries in the city.
He later said that this was the day that decided his career.

@words 1864 | He marries *Laura Spelman.*
A few months earlier, he had married Laura Spelman, a schoolteacher he had known since his school days. She was as serious, and as religious, as he was.
He once said that her judgement was always better than his.

# The Standard

@names Henry Flagler | Samuel Andrews | William Rockefeller
Rockefeller did not build alone. He brought in his brother William, and sent him to New York to handle exports.
And he brought in Henry Flagler, a bold and clever businessman, who became his closest partner.
For years the two men walked to work together, and home again, planning as they went.

@words Henry Flagler | A fortune *lost.* | A second chance.
Flagler had already made one fortune, and lost it, in the salt business. He brought money from a wealthy relative, and something more valuable. Nerve.
Many years later, he would spend his Standard Oil fortune building the railroads and hotels that created modern Florida.

@year 1870 | Standard Oil is founded
On the tenth of January, 1870, they created a new company in Ohio, with $1 million in capital.
They called it Standard Oil. Rockefeller was its president. He was thirty years old.

@stat | 2667 | | of the first 10,000 shares were his
The company issued 10,000 shares. Rockefeller held 2,667 of them, more than anyone else.

@words The name was a *promise.* | Kerosene of a standard quality | that customers could trust.
The name was a promise. In those days, badly refined kerosene could explode in the lamp.
Standard meant a product of a standard quality, that customers could trust.

@share 0 10 | of America's refining, in 1870
At its birth, Standard Oil handled about a tenth of America's oil refining.
The industry around it was a mess. There were far more refineries than the market needed, and most of them were losing money.

@duo Others saw;A crisis | He saw;An opening
Other refiners saw a crisis. Rockefeller saw an opening.
If one company could bring the whole industry under a single roof, the chaos would end. He intended it to be his.

@words He called it | *cooperation.* | His rivals had other words.
He did not think of this as greed. He truly believed that unlimited competition was destroying the industry, and that he was saving it.
He called his plan cooperation. His rivals would find other words for it.

@art barrel | Efficiency, taken to an extreme
His first weapon was efficiency, taken to an extreme.
Standard made its own barrels, and bought its own forests for the wood. It cut the cost of a barrel by more than half.
It owned its own wagons, its own warehouses, and before long, its own tank cars.

@art can | 40 drops of solder, or 39?
One story shows how his mind worked. Watching a machine seal cans of kerosene, he asked how many drops of solder it used. The answer was forty.
He asked the men to try thirty-eight. Some of those cans leaked. So they tried thirty-nine. None leaked.
Thirty-nine drops became the rule. One drop, on every can, saved thousands of dollars a year.

@names Paraffin wax | Lubricating oil | Petroleum jelly
Nothing was thrown away. What other refiners poured into the river, Standard turned into products.
Paraffin wax. Lubricating oil. Petroleum jelly.

@art train | Secret discounts: the rebates
His second weapon was the railroads.
Oil had to travel by rail, and Standard shipped more of it than anyone. Flagler went to the railroads with an offer.
Standard would guarantee sixty carloads of oil, every single day. In exchange, the railroad would quietly hand back part of the price. A rebate.

@words Cheaper for Standard. | More expensive | for *everyone else.*
For the railroad, it was steady, predictable business. For Standard, it meant moving oil more cheaply than any rival.
And the bigger Standard grew, the bigger the discounts became.

@year 1872 | The South Improvement Company
Then, in the winter of 1871 and 1872, came a far more aggressive plan. It had a dull name: the South Improvement Company.
A small group of refiners, led by Standard, made a secret pact with the biggest railroads.

@duo Rebates;Money back on;their own oil | Drawbacks;Money back on;their rivals' oil
Freight rates would roughly double for everyone outside the group. Members would get rebates on their own shipments.
And then the remarkable part. They would also be paid a share of what their competitors paid. These were called drawbacks.
In effect, every rival would be funding the company that was trying to destroy it.

@art newspaper | The Oil War of 1872
In February 1872, the new rates leaked. The oil region exploded with anger.
Producers held mass meetings, marched with torches, and refused to sell a drop of oil to anyone in the scheme.
Within weeks, the state of Pennsylvania cancelled the company's charter. It never shipped a single barrel.

@words But in *Cleveland,* | it was already | too late.
But in Cleveland, it was already too late.

@words *Sell* to Standard Oil. | Or try to *compete* with it.
While the scheme was still alive, Rockefeller visited his competitors in Cleveland, one by one.
He explained, politely, what was coming. And he made each of them an offer.
Sell to Standard Oil, for cash, or for shares. Or try to compete against a company that will always ship for less than you can.

@art ledger | He opened his books
Sometimes, he simply opened his books. He let a rival see what it cost Standard to make a gallon of kerosene, and to ship it.
One look at the numbers was usually enough.

@grid 26 22 | Cleveland refiners sold to Standard Oil
In a matter of weeks, twenty-two of his twenty-six Cleveland competitors had sold.
It became known as the Cleveland Massacre.

@duo Those who took cash;Often felt;cheated | Those who took shares;Became;very rich
He urged them to take shares, not cash. Most did not trust him enough.
Those who took the cash often felt they had been cheated. Those who took the shares became very rich.

@stat $ | 79000 | | paid to the widow Backus
One case followed him for decades. A widow, Mrs. Backus, sold her late husband's oil business to Standard for $79,000.
She later said she had been robbed, and the story was told across the country.
Rockefeller insisted that he had paid more than it was worth, and that he had urged her to take shares instead. Had she taken them, she would have been wealthy.

@words Even his own *brother* | never forgave him.
One of the firms that was squeezed belonged to his own younger brother, Frank. Frank never forgave him.
Years later, he testified against Standard Oil in public.

@share 10 25 | of America's refining, by the end of 1872
By the end of 1872, Standard Oil controlled roughly a quarter of the country's refining. And Rockefeller was only getting started.

@year 1873 | The Panic of 1873
Then the economy itself came to his aid. In 1873, a financial panic set off a long depression. Weak refiners went under, all across the country.
Rockefeller had cash, and he had credit. He kept buying.

# The Empire

@names Pittsburgh | Philadelphia | New York
From Cleveland, he moved on the other refining cities. Pittsburgh. Philadelphia. New York.
This time he worked in near total secrecy. He approached the strongest refiner in each city, and persuaded him to join.

@art document | Bought in secret
The firms he bought kept their old names, and their old managers. To the outside world, they still looked like independent companies.
Their competitors had no idea they were now facing Standard Oil. Some rivals sold out to what they thought was an ally against Rockefeller.

@words His fiercest *enemies* | became his | ablest *managers.*
He had a talent for turning enemies into partners. A young man named John Archbold had led the protests against him in the oil region.
Three years later, Archbold was working for Standard. He would one day run it.

@names Local price wars | Secret subsidiaries | A network of informers
Where persuasion failed, there were other methods.
If a rival sold kerosene in one town, Standard would cut its price in that town alone, and keep it high everywhere else, until the rival gave in.
It set up companies that pretended to be independent, and were secretly its own.
And, according to its critics, it paid railroad clerks to report on every barrel its competitors shipped.

@art train | The tank cars
It bought up most of the tank cars in the country. A rival who wanted to ship oil might find that there were none left to rent.

@duo To its rivals;Ruthless | To its own people;Loyal
And yet Standard was, by the standards of the day, a good employer. It paid well, and its workers rarely went on strike.
Rockefeller was ruthless toward competitors, and loyal to those inside.

@art pipeline | Then came the pipelines
But there was a threat. Pipelines could carry crude oil more cheaply than any train.
In 1877, the mighty Pennsylvania Railroad backed a pipeline company, and began buying refineries of its own. It was a direct challenge.

@duo Standard Oil;Pulled all its freight;and cut prices | The Pennsylvania Railroad;Bled money;then surrendered
Rockefeller answered with force. He pulled every barrel of his freight off the Pennsylvania Railroad, and cut prices wherever its refineries sold.
The railroad was losing a fortune. Then a violent strike paralysed its lines.
By the autumn, it surrendered, and sold its refineries and pipelines to Standard Oil.

@art pipeline | The Tidewater pipeline, 1879
Two years later, a group of independents tried again. They built a pipeline more than a hundred miles long, over the mountains, toward the sea. Experts said it could not be done. It worked.
Standard responded by building its own lines, thousands of miles of them, and buying into its rival. Within a few years, the pipelines were his as well.

@share 25 90 | of America's refining, by about 1880
By about 1880, Standard Oil refined roughly 90% of America's oil.
Rockefeller was barely forty years old.

@art document | The railroads' books are opened
In 1879, an investigation in New York State forced the railroads to open their books.
It showed that Standard had been receiving rebates on a scale nobody had imagined. Millions of dollars had been quietly handed back.

@art gavel | Indicted in Pennsylvania, 1879
The law was beginning to notice. In 1879, a grand jury in Pennsylvania indicted Rockefeller, and several of his colleagues, for conspiracy.
He simply stayed out of the state. The case was never tried.

@art scales | A legal problem
But the empire had a legal problem. Under the law of the time, a company chartered in one state could not simply own companies in another.
Standard was really dozens of separate firms, held together by handshakes, and by secrets.

@year 1882 | The Standard Oil Trust · nine trustees
His lawyer, Samuel Dodd, found the answer. In 1882, the shareholders of about forty companies handed their shares to nine trustees, led by Rockefeller.
In return, they received certificates in something new. It was called the Standard Oil Trust.

@words A word that once meant | faith and confidence | now meant *monopoly.*
On paper, no company owned any other. In practice, nine men in one room controlled an industry.
Other industries copied the idea. Sugar. Tobacco. Whiskey. And a word that once meant faith and confidence took on a new meaning. Monopoly.

@art building | 26 Broadway, New York
The trust moved into a new headquarters at 26 Broadway, in New York. It became one of the most famous business addresses in the world.
Every day the senior men ate lunch together, at one long table, and ran the company by committee.

@art barrel | Straight to the shopkeeper
Standard even changed how oil was sold. It sent its own horse-drawn tank wagons from town to town, delivering kerosene directly to the local shopkeeper, and cutting out the wholesalers in between.

@stat | 100000 | | employees, by the 1890s
By the 1890s, the trust employed about 100,000 people.

@words He rarely raised | his *voice.*
Rockefeller himself was not what people expected. He was quiet, polite, and almost never raised his voice.
He took naps. He left the office early. He listened far more than he spoke.
And he trusted his managers with a freedom that was rare for the time.

@words Four children. | *One* bicycle.
At home, he raised his children as if the family were poor. Four children shared a single bicycle, so that they would learn to share.
Each of them kept a small account book, as he had, and earned pennies for chores, like killing flies and sharpening pencils.

@art globe | The lamps of the world
His kerosene now lit lamps across the world. For years, most of the kerosene made in America was sold abroad.
It was carried up rivers in China, and across deserts by camel. Where people had no lamps, Standard sold them cheaply, to create customers for the oil.

@names Russian oil from Baku | The Nobels and the Rothschilds | Shell
But abroad, for the first time, he met real competition. Vast oil fields were found in Russia, at {Baku|Bah-koo}, developed by the Nobel and Rothschild families.
And a London merchant sent tankers through the Suez Canal, under the sign of a seashell. His company would become Shell.

@art refinery | The Lima gamble
At home, Rockefeller made one of his boldest bets. New oil fields had been found around {Lima|Lyma}, Ohio. But the oil was full of sulphur. It stank, and nobody could refine it.
His own board refused to buy it. Rockefeller offered to pay for it himself.
The board gave way. He hired a German chemist, who found a way to remove the sulphur. The worthless oil became a fortune.

@share 0 25 | of America's crude oil production, by about 1891
With that, Standard moved into the oil fields themselves. By about 1891, it was producing roughly a quarter of America's crude oil.
It now reached from the well, to the refinery, to the lamp.

@bars #¢ a gallon | 1870:26 | Mid 1880s:8
And here is the part that complicates the story. In these years, the price of kerosene fell, by well over half.
Light became cheaper for millions of ordinary people.

@duo His defenders;Order and low prices;in a wasteful industry | His critics;Every rival crushed,;in secret
Rockefeller's defenders said he had brought order, quality and low prices to a wasteful industry.
His critics said he had done it by crushing every rival, in secret, and that no single man should ever hold that much power.

@words Both were telling | the *truth.*
Both were telling the truth.

# The Reckoning

@art newspaper | The Atlantic Monthly, 1881
The first great attack came in print. In 1881, a journalist named Henry Demarest Lloyd published an article in The Atlantic Monthly, about Standard Oil and the railroads.
The issue sold out again and again. For the first time, the whole country was reading about the rebates.

@art gavel | Hearings and investigations
State governments began to investigate. Rockefeller was called to testify.
On the witness stand he was calm, courteous, and remarkably forgetful.

@year 1890 | The Sherman Antitrust Act
In 1890, Congress passed the Sherman Antitrust Act. It outlawed combinations that restrained trade. It was aimed squarely at companies like Standard.

@words The trust was *dissolved.* | On paper.
Two years later, a court in Ohio ordered the trust to be dissolved. Standard obeyed, slowly, and on paper.
The same men still owned the same companies, and still met in the same building.

@year 1899 | Standard Oil of New Jersey
Then the state of New Jersey changed its laws, to allow one company to own shares in others.
In 1899, the whole empire was reorganised under a single holding company. Standard Oil of New Jersey.

@words The most *hated* man | in America.
By now, Rockefeller had become the most hated man in America.
And yet he was hardly there. In the 1890s his health broke down, and he stopped going to the office.
John Archbold ran the company. But Rockefeller kept the title of president, so every attack still landed on him.

@words No hair. No eyebrows. | The face the public | loved to *hate.*
In 1901, an illness cost him all of his hair, even his eyebrows. Photographs of the thin, bald old man became the image the public loved to hate.

@art pen | Ida Tarbell, journalist
Then came the most dangerous critic of all. Her name was Ida Tarbell.
She had been born in the oil region of Pennsylvania, two years before Drake struck oil.
Her father was one of the independent oil men who had been squeezed by Standard's deals with the railroads. She never forgot it.

@names Napoleon | Abraham Lincoln | Standard Oil
By 1900, she was already one of the best known writers in America, famous for her biographies of Napoleon, and of Abraham Lincoln.
When she chose Standard Oil as her next subject, her own father begged her not to. He warned her that the company would ruin the magazine.

@words She did not rely | on *anger.* | She relied on *documents.*
Tarbell was a writer for McClure's Magazine. She did not rely on anger. She relied on documents.
Court records. Testimony. Company letters. She spent years piecing them together.

@art document | A source on the inside
She even found a source inside the company. Henry Rogers, one of Standard's most senior men, agreed to meet her, and went on talking to her for about two years.
He seems to have believed she would tell the company's side of the story.

@words Introduced by | *Mark Twain.*
The introduction was arranged by a friend they had in common. The writer Mark Twain.

@stat | 19 | | parts, in McClure's Magazine, from 1902
Beginning in November 1902, her history of Standard Oil ran in McClure's Magazine, in nineteen parts.
Month after month, she laid out exactly how the empire had been built. The rebates. The drawbacks. The secret purchases.
She was fair about Rockefeller's genius. She was merciless about his methods.

@words The public was *gripped.* | And *angry.*
The public was gripped. And angry.

@words He never answered her | in *public.*
Rockefeller never answered her in public. In private, he is said to have called her Miss Tar Barrel.
His silence, which had served him so well in business, now looked like guilt.

@art document | The Archbold letters, 1908
Worse was to come. In 1908, the newspaper owner William Randolph Hearst obtained letters taken from John Archbold's office, and read them out in public.
They showed Standard sending money to United States senators. The company's defenders could say very little.

@art court | President Theodore Roosevelt
In Washington, President Theodore Roosevelt saw his moment. He had built his name on taking on the great trusts, and Standard Oil was the greatest of them all.

@words "Malefactors | of *great wealth.*"
Roosevelt had a phrase for men like Rockefeller. He called them malefactors of great wealth.

@stat $ | 29240000 | | the fine, in 1907
In 1907, a federal judge fined one Standard company more than $29 million, for taking illegal rebates. It was the largest fine in American history at that time.
Rockefeller is said to have been playing golf when the news arrived. He read the message, put it in his pocket, and carried on with the game.
He predicted the fine would never be paid. He was right. It was overturned on appeal.

@words The judge later ran | *baseball.*
The judge, {Kenesaw|Kennesaw} Mountain Landis, would later become the first commissioner of baseball.

@art scales | United States v. Standard Oil
But the real danger was a different case. In 1906, the federal government had sued to break up Standard Oil itself, under the Sherman Act.
The case ran for years. Hundreds of witnesses. Thousands of pages of testimony.
In 1909, a federal court ruled against Standard. The company appealed to the Supreme Court.

@names Frank Kellogg, prosecutor | More than 400 witnesses | More than 1,300 exhibits
The government's case was led by a lawyer named Frank Kellogg, who would later win the Nobel Peace Prize.
He called more than four hundred witnesses. Rockefeller himself took the stand, and as always, he was calm, polite, and gave almost nothing away.

@words 15 May 1911 | An *illegal monopoly.* | Six months to break apart.
On the fifteenth of May, 1911, the Supreme Court gave its ruling.
Standard Oil was an illegal monopoly. It had six months to break itself apart.

@duo The court's test;Was the restraint;unreasonable? | Its answer;Yes.
The Chief Justice, Edward White, read the opinion. Not every large company was illegal, he said. Only those that restrained trade unreasonably. It became known as the rule of reason.
Standard Oil, the court found, had failed that test completely.

@split 34 | separate companies
It was split into thirty-four separate companies.

@share 90 65 | of American refining, by 1911
There is an irony here. By the time the court acted, Standard's grip was already slipping.
Huge new oil fields had been found in Texas, and new rivals had grown up around them.
Standard's share of the market had fallen from about 90% to roughly two thirds. Competition had already begun to do what the court now ordered.

# The Twist

@words And here | is the *twist.*
And here is the twist.

@split 34 quarter | About a quarter of every piece
Rockefeller owned about a quarter of Standard Oil. After the breakup, he owned about a quarter of each of the thirty-four pieces.
Nothing was taken from him. His shares were simply divided up.

@split 34 | Shares in every one
Every shareholder in the old company simply received shares in each of the new ones.

@words Buy | *Standard Oil.*
There is a story that Rockefeller was playing golf when the news of the ruling arrived. He is said to have turned to his partner, a priest, and asked whether he had any money.
If so, he advised, buy Standard Oil.

@art document | For the first time, the shares traded openly
And then something happened that almost nobody expected. For decades, Standard had told investors as little as possible. Nobody outside really knew what its parts were worth.
Now, the shares of the new companies began to trade openly. And investors discovered that the pieces were worth far more than the whole.

@bars $# million | 1911:300 | 1913:900
Within about a year, the shares of most of the new companies had doubled. Some had tripled.
By most estimates, Rockefeller's fortune rose from around $300 million to around $900 million.

@stat | 2 | % | of the entire American economy
That was roughly two percent of the entire American economy, in the hands of one man.
The breakup that was meant to punish him had made him the richest man in the country.

@words On Wall Street, | they prayed for | *another* breakup.
On Wall Street, the joke went round as a prayer. Please, send us another dissolution.

@art bulb | The electric light
There was a second piece of luck. For forty years, the oil business had been the business of light.
But in the 1880s, Thomas Edison's electric lamp had begun to replace kerosene. In the cities, the oil lamp was slowly dying.

@art car | A new demand: gasoline
And just as it faded, a new customer arrived. The automobile.
In 1908, Henry Ford launched the Model T. Suddenly, there was a huge new demand for a product that refiners had once treated as waste. Gasoline.
Around the time of the breakup, gasoline overtook kerosene as the oil industry's most important product.

@art refinery | The breakup set the companies free
And the breakup itself helped. Under the old Standard, one cautious headquarters decided everything.
A chemist at Standard Oil of Indiana had found a way to get far more gasoline out of every barrel of crude oil. Headquarters had refused to pay for it.
Once Indiana was independent, it built the process anyway. It transformed the industry.

@words Freed from one another, | the pieces *grew faster* | than the whole.
Freed from one another, the pieces began to compete, to experiment, and to grow.

@arrows Standard Oil of New Jersey > Exxon | Standard Oil of New York > Mobil | Standard Oil of California > Chevron
Those pieces are still with us.
Standard Oil of New Jersey became Exxon.
Standard Oil of New York became Mobil.
Standard Oil of California became Chevron.

@arrows Standard Oil of Indiana > Amoco | Continental Oil > Conoco | The Ohio Oil Company > Marathon
Standard Oil of Indiana became Amoco. Continental Oil became Conoco. The Ohio Oil Company became Marathon.

@words *S. O.* | Say it aloud: | *Esso.*
Some kept the old name alive in disguise. The brand Esso is simply the letters S and O, spoken aloud.

@stat | 3 | of 7 | of the "Seven Sisters" came from Standard Oil
Others were swallowed by rivals. Standard of Ohio and Amoco both ended up inside the British company BP.
For much of the twentieth century, the world's oil was dominated by seven giant companies, known as the Seven Sisters. Three of the seven were children of Standard Oil.

@year 1999 | Exxon and Mobil merge
And in 1999, the two largest pieces, Exxon and Mobil, merged back together, in a deal worth about $80 billion.
Nearly ninety years after the breakup, part of Standard Oil had put itself back together.

# The Giver

@words Money could not buy | a *good name.*
For all his wealth, Rockefeller could not buy a good name. And in 1914, it sank even lower.

@art newspaper | Ludlow, Colorado, 1914
The family controlled a coal company in Colorado. Its miners went on strike, and were thrown out of their company homes. They moved into a camp of tents, at a place called Ludlow.
In April 1914, state militia and company guards attacked the camp. About twenty people died, among them women and children.
The outrage fell on the Rockefeller name. It was the lowest point the family ever reached.

@words His son went to | *Colorado* | to face the miners.
It fell to his son, John D. Rockefeller Junior, to answer for it. At first, he defended the company. Then he changed course.
He travelled to Colorado, went down into the mines, ate with the miners, and listened to their complaints.
It did not undo what had happened. But it marked a change in how the family dealt with the public.

@words He had been *giving* | all his life.
And yet there was another side to the story, and it was not invented to repair his image. He had been giving money away since his first job.
The same ledger that recorded his first wages, also recorded his first gifts.

@art coins | Giving, run like a business
As his fortune grew, the requests grew with it. Thousands of letters came every month. People followed him in the street.
So he did what he had always done. He organised it. He hired a former minister, Frederick Gates, to run his giving like a business.

@words "Faster than | it *grows.*"
Gates gave him a warning. His fortune was rolling up like an avalanche. He would have to give it away faster than it grew, or it would crush him, and his children after him.

@duo Charity;Treat the;symptom | His approach;Find and remove;the cause
Gates and Rockefeller shared one idea. Do not simply hand out money to relieve suffering. Find the cause, and remove it.
They called it wholesale giving, not retail.

@art court | The University of Chicago
In 1890, he gave the money to found the University of Chicago. Over the years, he gave it about $35 million. He rarely visited, and he refused to let it carry his name.

@art church | Spelman College
He helped fund a college for Black women in Atlanta. It was renamed Spelman, after his wife's family, who had been active in the fight against slavery.

@art ledger | Schools across the South
He funded a board to improve schools across the American South, for Black and white students alike, at a time when few others would.

@art microscope | The Rockefeller Institute, 1901
In 1901, he founded the Rockefeller Institute for Medical Research, in New York. It was the first of its kind in America.
Its scientists went on to make discoveries that shaped modern medicine. Today it is called Rockefeller University.

@names A treatment for meningitis | The role of DNA
Its researchers produced an early treatment for meningitis. Decades later, it was there that scientists showed that DNA is the material that carries our genes.

@art globe | The Rockefeller Foundation, 1913
In the American South, his money paid for a campaign against hookworm, a parasite that left millions of people weak and ill.
And in 1913, he created the Rockefeller Foundation, with a mission as wide as he could make it. To promote the well-being of mankind, throughout the world.
It funded public health on every continent, including the research that produced a vaccine against yellow fever.

@names Rockefeller Center | Colonial Williamsburg | The land for the United Nations
The giving did not stop with him. His son spent most of his life giving the fortune away.
He built Rockefeller Center in New York, paid for the restoration of Colonial Williamsburg, and bought the land on which the United Nations now stands.

@art building | A medical school in Beijing
In China, it built one of the most advanced medical schools in Asia.

@stat $ | 540 |  million | given away in his lifetime
In all, he gave away around $540 million.
It did not buy forgiveness from everyone. Critics called it tainted money. But it changed how the very rich give.

@art coins | The man with the dimes
In old age, he became a different kind of public figure. A thin, smiling old man, who played golf every day, and handed out shiny new dimes to the people he met.
He is said to have given away tens of thousands of them.

@words Golf, every day. | A strict diet. | One goal: *one hundred.*
He lived by a strict routine. Golf every day. Plain food, in small amounts. Plenty of rest. He was still aiming for a hundred.

@year 1937 | Ormond Beach, Florida
John D. Rockefeller died in May 1937, in Florida. He was ninety-seven years old. He had missed his second ambition by a little under three years.

@stat $ | 26 |  million | left in his estate
His estate was worth about $26 million. Almost everything else had already been given away, to his children, and to his foundations.

# The Lesson

@words So what is the *lesson* | of Standard Oil?
So what is the lesson of Standard Oil? There are three.

@art bottleneck | One: control the narrow point
The first is about where power sits. Rockefeller did not win because he found oil. He never cared much for drilling.
He won because he controlled the narrow point that everyone else had to pass through.
The refineries. The railroads. The pipelines.

@words Control the *bottleneck,* | and you control | the industry.
Thousands of people could drill a well. Millions could buy a lamp. But between them, there was only one road, and he owned it.
Control the bottleneck, and you control the industry.

@art can | Two: the small numbers
The second lesson is about the small numbers. The single drop of solder. The cost of a barrel. The fraction of a cent.
Rockefeller's rivals chased the big strike. He counted the costs that nobody else bothered to count, and at his scale, those fractions became a fortune.

@art scales | Three: power has a price
The third lesson is a warning. The same secrecy that built the empire is what finally destroyed its reputation.
Standard Oil lowered the price of light for millions of people. But it did so in the dark, by methods it would not defend in public.
And when the public finally saw the documents, no amount of cheap kerosene could save it.

@art ledger | The habits in the ledger
And there is a quieter lesson, in the ledger he kept at sixteen. The habits that built Standard Oil were not dramatic.
Measure everything. Waste nothing. Keep cash for the moment when others have none. And be patient.
None of it is secret. And almost nobody does it.

@words No monopoly | lasts *forever.*
There is one more thing worth remembering. No monopoly lasts forever.
Before the Supreme Court ever ruled, new oil in Texas and a new light bulb in New Jersey had already begun to loosen Rockefeller's grip.

@duo One view;A predator who;rigged the market | Another view;A genius who built;the modern corporation
Historians still argue about Rockefeller. Some see a predator, who rigged a market in secret.
Others see a manager of genius, who built the first truly modern corporation, and made a vital product cheap.
The evidence supports both. That is what makes the story worth telling.

@art court | A law sharpened on Standard Oil
The case left a legal legacy too. For more than a century, when governments have gone after dominant companies, in telephones, in software, in technology, they have reached for the law that was sharpened on Standard Oil.

@timeline 1870:Founded | 1880:90% | 1911:Broken up | 1999:Reunited
An empire built in ten years. Dominant for thirty. Broken up by the highest court in the land. And still, in its pieces, among the largest companies on earth.

@words Who controls | the *bottleneck* | today?
So the next time one company seems to stand between everyone and something they need, it is worth asking the question that Ida Tarbell asked.
Not only, is it cheap? But, how was it built?
